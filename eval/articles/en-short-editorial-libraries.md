Where can you spend an afternoon indoors, in a heated or air-conditioned room, with a clean bathroom and fast internet, without buying anything? In most towns, the honest answer is the public library, and nowhere else.

That is why the debate over library budgets is about more than books. Circulation numbers have fallen at many branches over the past decade, and budget writers point to those numbers when they look for savings. But circulation measures only one of the things a library does. At the branch near my home, the busiest services on a weekday are the computers, the printers and the tables near the outlets. Job seekers fill out applications there. Students without quiet space at home do their homework there. Older residents come for the free classes on using a smartphone.

None of these people show up in circulation statistics, and many of them have no other place to go. A coffee shop expects you to buy something. A mall expects you to shop. A library expects nothing except that you be reasonably quiet.

Critics say libraries should charge for some services or cut hours that see little use. Some of that is fair: a branch that is empty on Sunday mornings does not need to open at 9 a.m. But the evening and weekend hours that get cut first are exactly the hours when working people can come.

Cities should measure libraries by visits and by the use of their spaces and computers, not only by checkouts, and fund them by what they actually provide. A town that cannot keep one free, warm, open room for its residents has decided something about who it is for.

The conclusion is simple: keep the doors open in the evenings and on weekends, even if it means fewer new books on the shelves.
