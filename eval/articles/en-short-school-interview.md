Ridgeview Middle School, a fictional public school with about 640 students, moved to a four-day school week in September as part of a two-year state pilot. Classes now run Monday through Thursday, each day 50 minutes longer than before. We spoke with principal Dana Okafor about the first two months.

Why did the school apply for the pilot?

"Mostly to hire and keep teachers. Last year we started with six open positions, and two of them were never filled. In the exit interviews, teachers kept telling us they were burned out. A three-day weekend is something we can offer that the district next door cannot."

Has it worked?

"It is too early to say. We filled every position this fall, and we had 41 applicants for the last science job, compared with three the year before. But one good hiring season does not prove much. The state will look at retention after the second year."

What about students?

"Attendance is up slightly, about one percentage point, but October always looks better than February. On Fridays we run an optional program with tutoring, art and sports. Around 230 students come on a typical Friday. We do not yet know whether the longer days hurt learning; the state tests in the spring will be the first real measure."

Some parents have complained about child care.

"Yes, and they are right to. For working parents, a free Friday is a problem, not a perk. The Friday program is free and runs until 3 p.m., but it is not full-day care, and we have a waiting list for the youngest grade. We are asking the district for money to extend it until 5 p.m., but nothing has been approved."

Is the school saving money?

"No. People assume a four-day week is about cutting costs. We save a little on buses and electricity, but the Friday program costs more than that. Our budget this year is almost exactly the same as last year's."

What happens if the results are bad?

"Then we go back to five days. The pilot was designed so that schools can leave after the first year, and the school board will vote on that in June."
