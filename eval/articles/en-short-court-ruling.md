A state appeals court ruled on Tuesday that QuickCart, a fictional grocery delivery app, must pay its couriers for the time they spend waiting at stores for orders, not only for the time they spend driving.

The three-judge panel upheld a 2025 decision by the state labor board, which found that about 11,000 QuickCart couriers in the state had been underpaid. The board had estimated that couriers spent an average of 14 minutes waiting per order and ordered back pay of $23 million. The appeals court agreed that waiting time is work time, but it sent the amount of back pay back to the board, saying the 14-minute average was based on a sample of only two cities.

QuickCart treats its couriers as independent contractors and pays them per delivery, plus a mileage rate. The ruling does not decide whether they are employees; that question is the subject of a separate case that is expected to go to trial next year.

"Today's decision says what couriers have been saying for years: standing in a store waiting for an order is work," said Maria Delgado, a lawyer for the couriers' association that brought the complaint.

QuickCart said it was "disappointed" and was considering an appeal to the state supreme court. A spokesperson said the company had already changed its app in March to show couriers the expected waiting time before they accept an order, and that average waiting times had fallen since then. The company did not give figures.

Labor law experts said the ruling could affect other delivery apps in the state, but disagreed on how much. "Most apps pay in a similar way, so the reasoning applies to them too," said Professor Alan Whitfield of Eastbrook University. Another expert noted that the decision applies only to waiting at the store, not to time spent waiting for the next order between deliveries.

The labor board has 90 days to recalculate the back pay.
