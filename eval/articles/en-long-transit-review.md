Twelve months ago, the Westmere Valley Regional Transit Authority made the biggest set of changes to its service since it was founded in 1974. It redrew almost every bus route in the region, cut fares for most riders, opened a four-station extension of its light rail line, and started replacing its diesel buses with battery-electric ones. The authority serves about 1.9 million people across the city of Westmere and eleven surrounding towns, and almost every one of them who rides transit has had to learn something new this year.

This review looks at each of those changes in turn, using the authority's own data, interviews with riders, drivers and planners, and the findings of an independent audit published last month. The short version: ridership has recovered faster than expected, the new bus network is working well in the dense core of the region and poorly in some outer suburbs, the rail extension is popular but over budget, and the electric bus program has run into problems with charging that the authority did not anticipate.

How ridership changed

In the year before the changes, the system carried an average of 182,000 trips on a weekday, still about 22 percent below its level before the pandemic. In the twelve months since, weekday ridership has averaged 214,000 trips, an increase of 18 percent. Weekend ridership grew even faster, by 27 percent, which planners attribute mostly to more frequent service on Saturdays and Sundays.

The growth was not even. Trips on the eight new "frequent corridors", where buses now come every ten minutes or better from early morning to late evening, rose by 34 percent. Trips on routes that kept roughly the same schedule rose by 6 percent, in line with the general recovery in travel. And on the routes in the outer suburbs where service was reduced or replaced, trips fell by 19 percent.

"The pattern is exactly what the research predicted," said Priya Natarajan, the authority's head of service planning. "When you make a bus come often enough that people stop checking the schedule, they use it for many more kinds of trips. When you make it less convenient, some people stop riding."

The authority's goal had been to return to pre-pandemic ridership by 2028. At the current pace, it could reach that level a year early. But Natarajan cautioned that part of this year's growth came from people trying the system after the fare cut, and that the authority will not know until next year how many of them stay.

The bus network redesign

The redesign was built around a choice that transit planners often describe as frequency versus coverage. A transit agency with a fixed budget can run buses often on a smaller number of busy streets, which attracts more riders, or it can spread buses thinly across the whole region, so that more people have some service near their home, even if it comes only once an hour.

Westmere chose to shift toward frequency. Before the redesign, about 30 percent of bus service hours went to routes that came every 15 minutes or better. Now it is 61 percent. The number of routes fell from 74 to 52, and many of the remaining routes are straighter, with fewer detours into parking lots and cul-de-sacs.

In the dense neighborhoods of central Westmere, the results have been visible. Average waiting time at bus stops, estimated from the authority's fare card data, fell from 11.5 minutes to 6.8 minutes. The share of riders who needed to transfer rose, from 38 percent to 47 percent of trips, but because the connecting buses come more often, the average total travel time for a trip between two points in the core fell by about 9 percent.

Riders in the core mostly approve. In the authority's rider survey, conducted twice a year with about 3,000 respondents each time, satisfaction with "how long I wait for the bus" rose from 41 percent to 63 percent in the central zone.

The outer suburbs tell a different story. In towns such as Ashby, Coldbrook and Pine Hollow, several low-ridership routes were replaced by an on-demand service: riders book a ride in a small van through an app or by phone, and the van picks them up within a zone and takes them to a transit hub or to another point in the zone. The authority promised that the average wait would be under 20 minutes.

It has not been. The average wait for an on-demand ride over the year was 31 minutes, and at peak times in the morning it often exceeded 45 minutes. The service has only 24 vans for an area of about 140 square miles, and demand turned out to be higher than the planners modeled, especially for trips to medical appointments and to the two community colleges in the area.

"I used to know my bus came at 7:42," said Marjorie Tull, a retired nurse in Coldbrook who uses the service to get to dialysis three times a week. "Now I book the night before and hope. Twice this year I missed my appointment." Her experience is common in the complaints the authority has received: on-demand service accounted for 9 percent of all trips but 37 percent of all complaints.

The authority's board voted in September to add 10 vans and to bring back a fixed route between Coldbrook and the Westmere Medical Center, running every 30 minutes on weekdays. Board chair Samuel Achebe acknowledged that the agency had underestimated how many trips in the suburbs were time-sensitive. "We designed on-demand for flexibility, and some people needed predictability more than flexibility," he said.

Fares: simpler and cheaper, with a gap

The second major change was to fares. The authority replaced a system of three fare zones, with prices from $2.25 to $4.50 per trip, with a single fare of $2.00 for every trip, including transfers within 90 minutes. It also introduced fare capping: once a rider has paid $5 in a day or $60 in a calendar month on the same card or phone, every further trip in that period is free.

The fare cut was paid for partly by a state grant of $38 million over three years, and partly by an expected increase in ridership. Fare revenue fell by 14 percent in the first year, less than the 21 percent the authority had budgeted for, because more trips were taken than expected.

Fare capping has been particularly important for lower-income riders, who often could not afford the up-front cost of a monthly pass under the old system and paid much more per trip as a result. The audit found that 41 percent of riders reached the monthly cap at least once in the year, and that riders who live in the region's lowest-income neighborhoods saved an average of $23 a month.

There is a gap, though. Fare capping works only for riders who pay with a reloadable card or a phone. Riders who pay with cash on the bus, about 8 percent of trips, still pay $2.00 every time and never reach a cap. Advocates for low-income riders have asked the authority to sell cards at more corner stores and to let riders load cash onto a card at the farebox, which the authority says it can do once the new fareboxes are installed in 2028.

The light rail extension

In March, the authority opened a 5.2-mile extension of the Green Line light rail from Harbor Street to the Eastgate area, with four new stations. It is the first rail expansion in the region in fifteen years.

Ridership on the extension has exceeded forecasts. The authority projected 11,000 weekday boardings at the four new stations in the first year; the actual average has been 14,600. The busiest new station, Eastgate Center, sits next to a large hospital and a university campus, and the authority credits those two destinations for much of the demand.

The construction did not go as smoothly. The extension cost $1.31 billion, compared with an original budget of $1.04 billion, an overrun of 26 percent. It also opened 14 months late. The audit traced most of the overrun to two causes: the relocation of underground utilities along Eastgate Avenue, which turned out to be more extensive than the authority's surveys had shown, and a dispute with the main contractor over the design of the elevated section near the river, which stopped work for five months.

The audit criticized the authority for signing a contract that left it responsible for most of the risk of unknown underground conditions, and recommended that future contracts include more thorough surveys before bidding and shared risk provisions. The authority's board accepted the recommendations.

The extension also changed travel patterns on the bus network. Three bus routes that used to run along Eastgate Avenue were shortened to feed the new stations instead of running parallel to the train, freeing about 40,000 service hours a year, which the authority moved to the frequent corridors.

Electric buses and the charging problem

The authority has committed to a fully zero-emission bus fleet by 2035. This year it put its first 60 battery-electric buses into service, out of a fleet of about 720 buses, and opened a charging depot in the Riverside neighborhood.

On paper, the electric buses have performed well. Energy costs per mile are about 55 percent lower than for diesel buses, and drivers generally like them: they are quieter and smoother to drive. Riders in the survey rated them higher for comfort.

In practice, the authority has struggled to keep them on the road. On an average weekday this year, only 44 of the 60 electric buses were available for service, compared with an availability rate of about 88 percent for the diesel fleet. The main problem has been charging. The Riverside depot has 30 chargers, enough in theory to charge the buses overnight. But two transformer failures in the summer took half of the chargers offline for a total of nine weeks, and the replacement parts took longer than expected to arrive.

Range has also been a problem in winter. The buses are rated for about 200 miles on a charge, but in cold weather, with the heating running, the authority found that some buses could only complete about 140 miles, not enough for the longest daily assignments. Planners had to reassign the electric buses to shorter routes in January and February, which is part of the reason the buses run mostly in central Westmere.

The authority has ordered 90 more electric buses for delivery over the next two years, and says it is changing its plans for the next depot as a result of this year's experience: it will include on-site battery storage to cover transformer failures, and a second, independent connection to the electricity grid. The cost of those changes, about $14 million, was not in the original budget.

Safety and cleanliness

Safety on transit has been a concern for riders across the country since the pandemic, and Westmere is no exception. In the rider survey, 52 percent of respondents said they felt safe on board at night, up from 46 percent a year earlier but still well below the 68 percent recorded in 2019.

The authority added 40 "transit ambassadors" this year: unarmed staff who ride trains and buses, answer questions, and call for help when needed. Reported incidents of harassment on the light rail fell by 17 percent on the lines where the ambassadors work most often. The authority also increased the frequency of station cleaning from once to twice a day at the 12 busiest stations.

Assaults on drivers remain a serious problem. There were 63 reported assaults on bus drivers in the year, up from 55 the year before. The drivers' union has asked for protective barriers on all buses; currently only about half the fleet has them. The authority says all new buses will come with barriers and that it will retrofit the remaining older buses by the end of next year.

Accessibility

The redesign raised particular concerns for riders with disabilities, because straighter routes with fewer detours can mean longer walks to a bus stop. The authority responded by keeping stops within a quarter mile of senior housing complexes and major medical facilities, and by adding 140 new benches and 85 new shelters along the frequent corridors.

The region's paratransit service, which provides door-to-door rides for people who cannot use regular buses, was not changed in the redesign. Paratransit trips rose by 9 percent over the year. The share of paratransit trips that picked up riders within the promised 30-minute window improved slightly, from 87 to 89 percent.

All four new rail stations have two elevators each, a response to complaints about older stations where a single broken elevator can make the platform unreachable for wheelchair users. Elevator availability across the whole rail system was 96.2 percent this year.

The workforce

None of the service increases would have been possible without enough drivers, and hiring was the authority's biggest worry going into the year. In 2024, it was short about 140 bus drivers and had to cancel around 4 percent of scheduled trips.

The authority raised starting wages for drivers by 12 percent, from $22.50 to $25.20 an hour, began paying trainees during their seven-week training period instead of only after they qualified, and opened a second training center. Over the year it hired 312 drivers, and the number of open driver positions fell to about 35. The share of scheduled trips that were cancelled fell to 0.9 percent.

Retention is improving too. The share of new drivers who left within their first year fell from 31 percent to 22 percent. Drivers interviewed for this review said the higher pay mattered, but so did changes to scheduling: the authority reduced the number of "split shifts", in which a driver works the morning rush and the evening rush with an unpaid break of several hours in between.

Finances

The authority's operating budget for the year was $612 million. About 18 percent came from fares, down from 23 percent before the changes; the rest came from a regional sales tax, state funding, and federal grants.

The authority ended the year with a small surplus of $9 million, mainly because fare revenue fell less than budgeted and because diesel prices were lower than expected. But its long-term picture is less comfortable. The state fare grant ends in two years. Federal relief funds from the pandemic, which covered part of the operating budget since 2021, have been spent. The authority projects an annual gap of about $70 million starting in 2029 unless it finds new revenue or cuts service.

The board has asked the region's voters to approve an increase in the transit sales tax of a quarter of a cent in next November's election, which would raise roughly $85 million a year. A poll commissioned by a local business group found 54 percent support for the increase, with 38 percent opposed.

How the redesign was planned

The changes did not come out of nowhere. Planning for the bus network redesign began in 2022, when the authority hired an outside consulting firm and asked the public a basic question: should the network aim mainly for high ridership, or mainly for coverage? The authority held 46 public meetings, ran an online survey that received about 21,000 responses, and published two draft networks for comment, one built for ridership and one built for coverage.

In the survey, 58 percent of respondents preferred a network closer to the ridership concept, 27 percent preferred the coverage concept, and the rest had no preference. The preferences were strongly divided by geography: in central Westmere, 71 percent favored ridership; in the outer suburbs, 61 percent favored coverage. The board adopted a compromise that moved about two thirds of the way toward the ridership concept and promised the on-demand service as a replacement for the weakest suburban routes.

Several board members now say the public process was thorough about the fixed routes but thin on the details of on-demand service. The draft networks showed on-demand zones as shaded areas on a map, without saying how many vans would serve them or how long riders would wait. "People in Coldbrook were told they would get something flexible and modern," said board member Teresa Lindgren, who represents several suburban towns. "Nobody told them it would be 24 vans for the whole area."

Bus lanes and street changes

Frequent buses only work if they are not stuck in traffic, and the authority could not achieve its goals alone. Most streets in the region are controlled by the city of Westmere or by the individual towns, and the authority had to persuade them to give buses priority.

The city of Westmere added 9.4 miles of bus-only lanes this year, mostly on the four busiest corridors, and changed traffic signals at 112 intersections so that a green light is held a few seconds longer when a bus is approaching. On the corridors with both bus lanes and signal priority, average bus speeds rose by 14 percent, and the variation in travel time from day to day fell by about a third, which means buses arrive closer to their scheduled times.

On the corridors without such changes, bus speeds did not improve, and in some cases got worse, because more frequent buses spent more time at crowded stops. The authority has asked the towns of Ashby and Marlow to add bus lanes on their main streets, but both town councils have so far declined, citing objections from businesses worried about losing parking.

Enforcement of the bus lanes is another problem. Cars parked or driving in bus lanes were the most common cause of delays reported by drivers. The city installed cameras on 30 buses this year that record vehicles blocking bus lanes, and issued about 18,000 warnings and 6,400 fines. The number of violations recorded per mile fell by 40 percent between the first and last quarter of the year.

Real-time information

A frequent network depends on riders trusting that the next bus will come soon, and a less frequent one depends on riders knowing exactly when it will come. Both depend on good information.

The authority replaced its real-time bus tracking system this year. The old system updated bus locations every 90 seconds and was often wrong by several minutes; the new one updates every 10 seconds. In tests, the predicted arrival times on the new system were within one minute of the actual arrival 84 percent of the time, compared with 52 percent for the old system.

The authority also installed 200 new arrival screens at bus stops along the frequent corridors, and launched a new trip planning app. The app has been downloaded about 160,000 times and is used for about 30 percent of on-demand bookings. Riders who do not use smartphones can get arrival times by sending a text message with the stop number, a service used about 40,000 times a month, or by calling a phone line.

Fare enforcement

The move to a single, lower fare was accompanied by a change in how fares are checked. On the light rail, where riders do not pass a driver, inspectors check tickets on board. The authority shifted from fines to warnings for first-time offenders and created a reduced-fare program for riders with low incomes, who now pay $1.00 per trip and have a monthly cap of $30.

About 26,000 riders enrolled in the reduced-fare program by the end of the year, roughly half the number the authority estimates are eligible. Enrollment requires proof of income, and advocates say the paperwork deters many people. The authority plans to enroll riders automatically if they are already enrolled in certain state benefit programs, which would require a data-sharing agreement with the state.

The share of light rail riders found without a valid fare during inspections was 4.1 percent this year, compared with 5.3 percent the year before. Inspectors issued 9,800 warnings and 2,100 fines.

Housing near transit

Transit planners often say that the best transit system in the world will not attract riders if there is nothing near the stations. The light rail extension was paired with zoning changes in the Eastgate area that allow apartment buildings of up to eight stories within a half mile of the new stations, where previously only single-family houses and low commercial buildings were allowed.

Since the zoning change two years ago, developers have applied for permits for about 2,300 apartments near the four new stations, and about 600 are under construction. The city requires that 15 percent of the units in larger buildings be rented at prices affordable to households earning less than 60 percent of the area's median income.

Some longtime residents of Eastgate worry that the new development will raise rents and push them out. A tenants' group has asked the city to add protections against eviction for existing renters near the stations. The city council is expected to vote on a proposal in the spring.

The environmental picture

One of the main arguments for investing in transit is that it reduces car trips and the emissions that come with them. Measuring that effect precisely is difficult, but the authority's consultants estimated that the increase in ridership this year replaced about 52 million miles of car travel, avoiding roughly 19,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions.

The electric buses added to that benefit. Each electric bus that replaces a diesel bus avoids about 90 metric tons of carbon dioxide per year, depending on how the electricity is generated. The region's electricity comes about 45 percent from renewable sources, a share that is expected to rise.

The authority's own emissions also fell, by about 6 percent, because the electric buses replaced some of the oldest and least efficient diesel buses in the fleet. The authority's goal is to cut its emissions in half by 2030, compared with 2019.

How Westmere compares

Westmere's experience is broadly similar to that of other mid-sized regions that redesigned their bus networks in recent years. Most saw ridership gains in the range of 10 to 25 percent on the frequent routes in the first two years, and most faced complaints from riders in areas where service was cut.

Where Westmere differs is in the size of its fare cut and the use of on-demand service as a replacement for fixed routes in the suburbs. Some other regions that tried on-demand service on a similar scale have since scaled it back, finding it expensive per trip: the authority's own figures show that an on-demand trip costs about $28 to provide, compared with about $6 for the average bus trip.

That cost is one reason the board decided to bring back a fixed route between Coldbrook and the medical center, rather than only adding vans. If a route has enough steady demand, planners say, a regular bus becomes cheaper per trip than on-demand vans, and more predictable for riders.

What riders say

Beyond the numbers, the riders interviewed for this review described a system that has become more useful for some people and less useful for others.

"For the first time, I don't own a car," said Diego Marín, a software tester who lives near one of the new frequent corridors. "The 12 comes every eight minutes. I just walk out and go." Several riders described similar decisions, and the authority's survey found that 6 percent of respondents had sold a car or decided not to buy one because of the new service.

Others feel left behind. In the outer suburbs, several riders said the redesign seemed to have been made for people in the city. "They took our bus and gave us an app," said Ray Donnelly of Pine Hollow, who does not own a smartphone and books the on-demand service by phone, often waiting on hold.

Drivers' views are mixed as well. Many like the simpler routes and say buses are more full, which makes the job feel more worthwhile. But some say more crowded buses on the frequent corridors bring more conflicts with riders, and the union has said that the authority has not added enough staff to clean and maintain the larger fleet.

Night and early-morning service

One group of riders gained more from the redesign than almost any other: people who work at night or very early in the morning. Before the changes, most bus routes stopped running around 11 p.m. and did not start again until 5:30 a.m., which left hospital staff, cleaners, warehouse workers and restaurant employees with few options.

The redesign added an overnight network of six routes that run every 30 minutes between midnight and 5 a.m., all meeting at the downtown transit center at the top and bottom of each hour so that riders can transfer without a long wait. The overnight routes carried an average of 2,900 trips a night this year. That is a small share of the system's total, but the authority's survey found that 70 percent of overnight riders had no car available for the trip.

"Before, I paid for a taxi home three nights a week," said Fatima Haddad, a cook at a downtown hotel whose shift ends at 1 a.m. "That was more than a hundred dollars a week. Now it's a bus and my monthly cap."

Overnight service is expensive to run per rider, and some board members have questioned whether it should be cut if the sales tax measure fails. Natarajan said the overnight routes would be among the last services the authority would consider cutting, because the people who use them have so few alternatives.

What the planners would do differently

Asked what they would change if they could start the year over, the planners and managers interviewed for this review gave remarkably consistent answers.

First, they would have tested the on-demand service on a smaller scale before relying on it in the whole outer suburban area. A six-month pilot in a single town, several said, would have revealed the demand for medical trips and the problems with phone booking before thousands of riders depended on the service.

Second, they would have started the electric bus program with a smaller number of buses on routes chosen for the shortest daily distances, and built backup power at the depot from the start. "We treated charging like fueling," said Marcus Webb, the authority's director of maintenance. "It is more like running a small power utility. We learned that the hard way in July."

Third, they would have spent more time explaining transfers. Many riders who had never had to change buses now do so on most trips, and the authority received thousands of questions in the first weeks about where to wait for a connecting bus. The authority eventually added signs at major transfer points showing which buses stop where, and staff at the eight busiest transfer points during the first month. Several planners said those measures should have been ready on the first day.

Finally, several people said the authority should have set clear measures of success for each part of the redesign before it began, and published them. The independent audit made the same point: without targets agreed in advance, it said, the public cannot easily judge whether a change worked, and the authority can be tempted to emphasize the numbers that look good.

How this review was done

This review draws on the authority's ridership data, which counts trips using fare card taps and automatic passenger counters installed on about 80 percent of buses and all trains. Counts for buses without counters are estimated from similar routes. Waiting times were estimated from the timing of fare card taps compared with the scheduled and actual arrival of buses at each stop, a method the authority's analysts have used since 2021.

The rider survey figures come from the authority's twice-yearly survey, which uses a random sample of riders approached on board. The response rate was about 34 percent. Results are weighted by route and time of day to match the overall population of riders. Figures on the extension's cost and schedule come from the independent audit by the state's auditor general, published last month.

Interviews were conducted in person and by phone with 41 riders, 12 drivers, six board members and nine staff members of the authority. Riders were recruited at stops and stations in eight areas of the region, including all three suburban areas with on-demand service.

What comes next

The authority's plans for the next two years include adding the vans and the fixed route in the outer suburbs; extending frequent service to two more corridors, along Mill Road and Lakeside Drive; installing new fareboxes that accept cash for card loading; and beginning environmental review for a second rail extension, to the airport.

It also faces several decisions without easy answers. If voters reject the sales tax increase, the authority will need to cut service, and it has not said where. The electric bus program needs more money for charging reliability. And the board will have to decide whether to keep the single $2 fare when the state grant ends.

Natarajan, the planning chief, said the past year had confirmed the basic bet of the redesign, but also its limits. "Frequency works where there are enough people to fill the buses. In places where homes are spread out, we need to be honest that transit will look different, and we need to make it reliable, even if it can't be frequent."

The authority will publish its next rider survey in February and will hold public meetings on the suburban service changes in January.
