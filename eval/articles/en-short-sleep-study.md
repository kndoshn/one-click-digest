People who took a 20-minute nap after studying a list of words remembered more of them the next day than people who stayed awake, according to a study of 96 adult volunteers published this week by researchers at the fictional Westmere Institute for Sleep Research.

The volunteers, aged 22 to 61, studied a list of 60 unrelated word pairs at 1 p.m. Half then napped in a quiet room for 20 minutes while wearing a headband that recorded brain activity; the other half watched a nature documentary for the same time. The next morning, the nap group correctly recalled an average of 41 pairs, compared with 35 for the group that stayed awake.

The researchers found that the benefit was largest for volunteers whose recordings showed bursts of brain activity called sleep spindles, which have been linked to memory in earlier studies. Volunteers who did not reach that stage of light sleep did about as well as the awake group.

Lead author Dr. Hana Lindqvist cautioned that the study was small and that it tested only one kind of memory: remembering word pairs. "We cannot say from this whether a nap helps you learn a language, a musical instrument or the material for an exam," she said. The study also excluded people with sleep disorders and people who work night shifts.

Longer naps did not appear to help more. In a smaller follow-up with 30 volunteers, a 60-minute nap produced roughly the same benefit as the 20-minute nap, and several volunteers reported feeling groggy afterwards.

The team plans a larger study next year with 400 volunteers in three cities, which will also test whether the timing of the nap matters.
